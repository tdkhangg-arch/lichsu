[SCENE 4: Father leans in.]

### 0:35 – Hook

[SCENE 5: The wide, flat, dusty plain at sunrise. Far away, two dark lines of soldiers face each other. On screen: Gaugamela, 1 October 331 BC]

October 1st, 331 BC. A dusty plain in northern Mesopotamia, in what is now Iraq. Two armies face each other in the morning sun, and the fate of the largest empire on Earth is about to be decided in a single day.

[SCENE 6: The Persian army: rows of soldiers in purple and saffron, a war elephant and a scythed chariot in front.]

On one side, the Persian Empire, the superpower of the ancient world. Its king, Darius the Third, has gathered soldiers from three continents: armored horsemen, elite guards, war elephants, and chariots with blades fixed to their wheels.

[SCENE 7: Alexander on his black horse Bucephalus in front of the Macedonian phalanx, pikes raised. On screen: Alexander, age 25]

On the other side, a young king from Macedonia, a small kingdom north of Greece, with about forty-seven thousand men. Outnumbered, far from home, with no road back. His name is Alexander.

### 1:23 – The road to Gaugamela

[SCENE 8: Map: Alexander's route from Macedonia across Asia Minor (Granicus, Issus), down to Egypt and back up to Gaugamela. On screen: 334–331 BC]

Three years earlier, Alexander had crossed into Asia. He beat a Persian army at the Granicus River, then beat Darius himself at Issus, where the Persian king fled the field. Then Alexander swung south and took Egypt without a fight.

[SCENE 9: Darius in his purple robe and tall tiara, between two Immortal guards. On screen: Darius III]

Darius offered peace: a fortune in silver, his daughter's hand in marriage, and every land west of the Euphrates River. Alexander said no. He didn't want half of Persia. He wanted all of it.

[SCENE 10: Darius, angry, points ahead; his guards behind him.]

So Darius prepared for one last, decisive battle. And this time, he would choose the ground.

### 2:02 – Darius sets a trap

[SCENE 11: Battle map: the long Persian line, a row of chariots in front, the open ground smoothed flat. On screen: A field leveled for chariots]

He picked the wide, open plain of Gaugamela. Then he ordered his men to flatten it, cutting down bushes, leveling bumps and clearing stones, so his chariots could charge at full speed.

[SCENE 12: Close-up of a scythed chariot racing across the flat ground, long blades on the wheels.]

These were scythed chariots: horse-drawn carts with long curved blades sticking out from the wheels. Darius had about two hundred of them, and a field as smooth as a racetrack.

[SCENE 13: Battle map: the Persian line is far longer than the Macedonian one; dashed purple arrows curl around both ends. On screen: Surround them]

His line stretched for miles, far longer than Alexander's. The plan was simple: wrap around both ends of the Macedonian army and crush it from every side.

[SCENE 14: Infographic on parchment: a small Macedonian crowd (47,000) next to a much bigger Persian crowd (100,000+?). On screen: How many Persians?]

How many men did Darius have? Ancient writers claimed up to a million, which is almost certainly an exaggeration. Modern historians usually guess around a hundred thousand. Either way, the Macedonians were badly outnumbered.

[SCENE 15: A war elephant with a Persian rider towers over the soldiers.]

He even brought fifteen war elephants from India. Any general in the world would have looked at that wall of men and lost his nerve. Almost any general.

### 3:08 – The night before

[SCENE 16: Night sky over the plain; the moon glows dark red during an eclipse. On screen: 20 September 331 BC]

Eleven nights before the battle, the moon turned a dark red in a lunar eclipse. Both armies saw it as an omen.

[SCENE 17: The clay tablet from Babylon, with a translated line about the battle.]

And we know the exact dates, because astronomers in Babylon recorded the eclipse on a clay tablet that still survives today. The same tablet mentions the battle itself, and the king whose troops deserted him.

[SCENE 18: From a dark hill, Macedonian soldiers look at thousands of Persian campfires across the plain. On screen: 30 September, night]

On the night of September 30th, the two armies camped just a few miles apart. The Macedonians could see the Persian campfires glowing across the whole plain, like a city of lights.

[SCENE 19: Inside Alexander's tent. Old general Parmenion, worried, pointing toward the enemy fires; Alexander at the map table. On screen: Parmenion]

Parmenion, Alexander's oldest and most experienced general, had an idea: attack now, at night, while the enemy can't see how few we are.

[SCENE 20: Alexander turns to Parmenion, calm and proud. On screen: “I will not steal my victory.”]

Alexander refused. According to the ancient writers, he answered: “I will not steal my victory.” A win in the dark could be blamed on luck. He wanted to beat Darius in broad daylight, so no one could ever say the Persians weren't truly beaten.

[SCENE 21: Father and son step into the tent and talk (son asks).]

SON: Okay, that sounds cool, but isn't it a dumb risk? A night attack sounds smart.

[SCENE 22: Father and son in the tent (father explains).]

FATHER: Honestly? Parmenion's plan had a real chance. But night attacks back then were chaos: men got lost, units ran into each other. And Alexander had a bigger goal. If Darius lost a fair fight, nobody in Persia could argue he still deserved to be king.

[SCENE 23: Persian soldiers standing stiffly in full armor in the cold night, tired and shivering.]

Darius, meanwhile, was afraid of exactly that night attack. So he kept his whole army standing in formation, in their armor, all night long. By sunrise, they were exhausted.

[SCENE 24: Alexander fast asleep, sitting slumped against the tent pole, smiling. Parmenion leaning in to wake him.]

And Alexander? He slept so deeply that his generals had to wake him in the morning. When they asked how he could sleep at a time like this, he reportedly said the hard part was over: Darius had finally agreed to fight.

### 5:14 – Battle lines

[SCENE 25: Battle map: the Macedonian army. Parmenion on the left, the phalanx in the center, Alexander and the Companion Cavalry on the right, a second line behind. On screen: Dawn, 1 October]

At dawn, Alexander set up his army. In the center stood the phalanx. On the right, Alexander himself with his elite Companion Cavalry. On the left, his general Parmenion. And behind them, a second line, ready to turn around if the Persians got behind them.

[SCENE 26: Close-up of the phalanx with sarissas lowered: rows of pike points sticking far out in front. On screen: The phalanx]

The phalanx was the backbone of the army. Each man carried a sarissa, a pike almost six meters long. With several rows of points sticking out in front, it was a moving hedge of iron that nobody wanted to walk into.

[SCENE 27: Battle map: Alexander's wing slides sideways to the right, at an angle. On screen: Alexander moves right]

Then Alexander did something strange. Instead of charging forward, he led his right wing sideways, to the right, at an angle. Step by step, he was pulling the battle off the smooth ground Darius had prepared.

[SCENE 28: Darius in his chariot, alarmed, pointing to his left; a Persian rider gallops off.]

Darius saw the danger. If the Macedonians got off his leveled field, his chariots would be useless. So he sent horsemen from his left wing racing out to block Alexander and get around him.

### 6:22 – The chariot charge

[SCENE 29: Scythed chariots thunder across the flat plain, dust behind them. On screen: The chariot charge]

Then Darius played his big card. The scythed chariots charged straight at the Macedonian line.

[SCENE 30: The phalanx steps aside and opens a lane; a chariot rushes harmlessly through the gap. On screen: Open the lanes!]

But Alexander was ready. Light troops in front aimed at the drivers and grabbed the horses' reins. And when the chariots reached the phalanx, the soldiers simply stepped aside, opening lanes. The chariots raced harmlessly through the gaps, where the troops behind them pulled the drivers down.

[SCENE 31: Father and son step onto the battlefield and talk (son asks).]

SON: Wait, they just... moved out of the way?

[SCENE 32: Father and son on the battlefield (father explains).]

FATHER: That's the whole secret. It sounds easy, but imagine standing still while blades come at you, and stepping aside at exactly the right second, all together. That takes years of drill. Darius had the fancy weapon. Alexander had the discipline.

### 7:10 – The gap

[SCENE 33: Battle map: the Persian left wing has drifted right after Alexander; a gap opens in the Persian line, right next to Darius. On screen: The gap]

Meanwhile, Darius kept sending more and more horsemen to the left to stop Alexander's sideways march. And every unit he sent stretched his own line thinner. Then it happened: a gap opened in the Persian line, right in front of Darius himself.

[SCENE 34: Close on Alexander on Bucephalus, eyes fixed on the gap.]

This was the moment Alexander had been waiting for. He stopped moving right and gathered his Companion Cavalry into a huge wedge, like an arrowhead, with himself at the tip.

[SCENE 35: Battle map: a glowing gold arrow from Alexander's wedge into the gap, straight at Darius; the phalanx pushes forward behind it. On screen: Charge!]

Then he turned and charged straight into the gap, aiming at one man: Darius. Right behind him, the phalanx lowered its pikes and pushed forward like a moving wall.

[SCENE 36: The Companion Cavalry gallops in a wedge, Alexander at the tip, lances leveled, dust behind.]

The Persian guards in the center tried to close the gap, but it was too late. The wedge was already inside.

[SCENE 37: Father and son on the battlefield as the cavalry storms past behind them (son asks).]

SON: He went straight for the king? The leader charging in front? Isn't that crazy?

[SCENE 38: Father and son on the battlefield (father explains).]

FATHER: Crazy risky, yes. But also smart. In an army like Persia's, everything depended on the king. Every order came from him. Scare him off the field, and the whole machine stops working.

### 8:24 – The king runs

[SCENE 39: Our drawing of the Alexander Mosaic from Pompeii: Alexander charging bareheaded, Darius in his chariot turning to flee.]

A famous ancient mosaic from Pompeii shows a moment just like this: Alexander charging bareheaded, and Darius in his chariot, turning away with terror on his face. Historians still argue whether it shows Gaugamela or Issus, but it shows how the ancient world remembered these two men.

[SCENE 40: Darius's golden chariot turns and flees across the plain; Darius looks back in fear.]

As the Macedonians fought their way closer, the driver beside Darius was struck down. The King of Kings turned his chariot around and fled the battlefield. For the second time, Darius ran from Alexander.

[SCENE 41: Persian soldiers look around in shock and start to run.]

The news spread across the plain like an earthquake. The king is gone! Persian soldiers who were still fighting, even winning in some places, lost heart. They began to fall back, and then to run.

[SCENE 42: Battle map: Persian horsemen press Parmenion on the left; a gold arrow shows Alexander turning back to help. On screen: Parmenion in trouble]

Not everything went Alexander's way. On the far left, Parmenion was in serious trouble, surrounded by Persian horsemen, and he sent riders begging for help. So Alexander gave up the chase and turned back to rescue his old general.

[SCENE 43: Sunset over the plain. Alexander on Bucephalus, his tired army behind him.]

By sunset, it was over. The greatest army of the East had been broken on the very field it had chosen.

### 9:41 – What came next

[SCENE 44: Alexander rides through the blue Ishtar Gate of Babylon; people welcome him. On screen: Babylon, 331 BC]

Weeks later, Alexander rode into Babylon, one of the richest cities on Earth, and its people came out to welcome him. Soon the royal city of Susa opened its gates too, and within months Persepolis had fallen.

[SCENE 45: Darius alone in the dark, his tiara gone, head down. On screen: 330 BC]

And Darius? He kept running. Within a year he was gone, betrayed and struck down by his own nobles. The empire his family had ruled for more than two hundred years was finished.

[SCENE 46: Map: Alexander's whole route, from Macedonia through Egypt and Persia to India. On screen: Alexander's empire]

Alexander kept marching, all the way to India. In about ten years, he built one of the largest empires the world had ever seen, and spread Greek language and culture from Egypt to the edge of India.

[SCENE 47: Parchment timeline of Alexander's life, Gaugamela highlighted.]

He never lost a major battle. He died young, at just thirty-two. And at Gaugamela, the battle that ended an empire, he was only twenty-five.

[SCENE 48: Father and son in front of the Ishtar Gate (son asks).]

SON: So the lesson is that numbers don't matter?

[SCENE 49: Father and son in front of the Ishtar Gate (father gives his take).]

FATHER: Numbers matter a lot. Most of the time, the bigger army wins. But Gaugamela shows what can beat them: a clever plan, soldiers drilled to perfection, and a leader who spots the one moment that counts, and doesn't hesitate.
